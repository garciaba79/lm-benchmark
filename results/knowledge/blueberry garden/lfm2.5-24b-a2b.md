## Pests to Watch For

**Aphids** — You're already treating these with neem oil, which is good. Check undersides of leaves regularly, especially on new growth where infestations start. The neem oil works by disrupting their life cycle; it's a preventative treatment as much as a cure.

**Spidermites** — These thrive in dry conditions and can show up when watering is inconsistent or the season is warm and low-humidity. Signs: fine webbing on leaf undersides, tiny yellow stippling (tiny dots where mites have fed). If you spot them, a strong water spray to dislodge them from leaves plus continued neem oil applications every 7–10 days until gone should manage it.

**Birds** — You've got netting covered. Beyond that: keep the area around the beds clear of other fruiting plants or berry bushes that might lure birds away, and check the net for trapped birds each morning.

**Deer/Rabbits** — If you're in an area with heavy wildlife pressure, consider a tall fence (at least 8 feet) or tree guards on young canes. Blueberries are palatable to them.

---

## Diseases to Monitor

**Powdery Mildew** — White flourish dust on the underside of leaves, especially in humid, poorly ventilated conditions. Improved airflow through light pruning and avoiding overhead watering helps. If it appears, a sulfur-based spray or a neem oil application can manage it.

**Botrytis (Gray Mold)** — Dark, water-soaked spots that turn gray and rotting on ripe fruit, usually after wet weather or poor drainage. Your netting already keeps berries off the ground; avoid any overhead moisture by keeping the drip irrigation at soil level.

**Root Rot** — Blueberries have shallow roots and need excellent drainage. Your raised beds with acidic mix should minimize this risk. If you see general yellowing or stunted growth, a soil pH test will tell you if the acidity is going too far (the roots won't function well outside the 4.5–5.5 range).

---

## Watering Consistently

Blueberries have shallow root systems and need steady moisture:

- **Frequency:** In raised beds with acidic organic mix, water once daily during hot, dry spells; every 2–3 days is usually enough in cooler or humid weather.
- **Volume:** Aim for about ½ inch of water per week from all sources (drip + rain). During peak summer, you may need to increase the frequency rather than the volume.
- **Method:** Keep the drip line at the root zone. Water at the base—never wet the leaves or berries, which encourages fungal issues.
- **Check:** Stick your finger about an inch into the soil near the plant; if it feels dry, water. The raised bed mix holds moisture well but can also dry quickly in summer heat.

---

## Fertilizer for Next Month

**Ammonium sulfate is a reasonable choice**, and you're doing so right by planning to apply it next month. For blueberries specifically:

- **Rate:** About ½ cup of ammonium sulfate per plant, scattered around the drip line (the area under the outer branches), then water it in well with your irrigation system.
- **Why it fits:** Ammonium is slightly acidic, so it maintains the soil pH that blueberries prefer. Sulfur supports healthy foliage and root development.

**Alternative / Complementary options:**

- **Blueberry-specific slow-release fertilizer** (e.g., Espoma Blue Berry-tone or Jack's Classic Blueberry Fertilizer): These are formulated for acid-loving plants, provide a gentler, longer nutrient release, and typically contain ammonium sulfate plus organic matter. They're less likely to cause root burn.
- **Organic option:** A light application of composted pine bark (already on hand) can add some nutrients over time without risking pH swings.

**A few cautions:**

- Blueberries are **light feeders**. Don't over-fertilize—excess nitrogen produces lots of leaves and very little fruit. Your goal is a moderate feeding, not heavy. Once per year in your second growing season onward should be sufficient.
- If you use ammonium sulfate again later, keep the same ½ cup rate.

Here are ten essential tips for starting a successful blueberry garden from scratch:

---

**1. Confirm and maintain acidic soil before planting.** Blueberries thrive in pH 4.5–5.5; no other common fertilizer or soil amendment can reliably fix alkaline soil. Test your site first. If it's too high, incorporate elemental sulfur well ahead of time (6+ months), or simply use raised beds with an acid-loving mix from day one.

**2. Choose three different varieties for staggered harvests.** Emerald, Jewel, and Star are solid choices—they differ in ripening time, flavor, and bush habit. Planting at least two is wise; planting three ensures you'll have berries available across a wider window of the season rather than all at once.

**3. Give them full sun.** Blueberries need at least 6–8 hours of direct sunlight daily for strong fruiting. Avoid shading from trees or structures. If your raised beds are on a north-facing slope, that often works well in northern climates.

**4. Water consistently because blueberries have shallow roots.** In raised beds with acidic organic mix they'll need deep watering roughly once every 2–3 days during hot summer weeks (daily if the soil dries out quickly). Use drip irrigation at the base and keep the root zone evenly moist—don't let it dry completely, but don't leave it waterlogged either.

**5. Mulch with acidic organic material.** Pine bark mulch is a great choice: it conserves moisture, suppresses weeds, and slowly adds to the acidity of the surface layer. Keep the mulch 2–3 inches thick but never let it pile up against the stems or touch the roots directly.

**6. Prune light in year one for structure, then annually afterward.** When the bushes are young, prune lightly in late winter—remove dead or broken wood and keep about three to four strong canes per plant. In subsequent years, prune each bush to maintain an open center with 3–4 fruiting arms: cane from the current season's growth won't bear fruit; only the previous year's canes set berries.

**7. Fertilize once a year in early spring.** For blueberries that are established past their first or second year, one application of ammonium sulfate (½ cup per plant, scattered around the drip line and watered in) is ideal. A slow-release blueberry-specific fertilizer works just as well if you prefer organic input.

**8. Protect from birds with netting.** This is non-negotiable for a harvest—berries left on the bush attract every bird within city limits. Drape net over the entire plant canopy, securing it to stakes or a frame so it doesn't sag into the fruit. Check each morning for trapped wildlife until the berries ripen.

**9. Stay ahead of pests with preventive care.** Watch for aphids (treat with neem oil if they appear) and spidermites (dislodge with a strong water spray, then follow up with neem oil). Keep foliage dry to prevent fungal issues like powdery mildew—your drip irrigation at ground level helps. If deer or rabbits are a problem in your area, keep them out with fencing.

**10. Be patient—the first berry crop takes two to three years.** Blueberries are slow to establish. Your new bushes may produce minimal fruit the first season, and significant harvests typically don't appear until you're at least halfway through the third growing year after planting. Stick to your care routine: consistent moisture, mulch, light feeding, and protection—and by year 3 you should have plenty for fresh eating, jam, and freezing.
