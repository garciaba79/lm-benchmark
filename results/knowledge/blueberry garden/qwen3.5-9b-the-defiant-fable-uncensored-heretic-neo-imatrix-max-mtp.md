Here’s a focused, practical guide tailored to your setup.

1. Improve growth and yield (general)
- Spacing & pruning:
  - Keep bushes 3–4 ft apart in the row; rows 6–8 ft apart for airflow.
  - In late winter/early spring, remove dead/diseased canes and thin out weak or crossing canes to keep an open center. Aim for 10–15 strong canes per plant over time.
- Soil & pH:
  - Maintain soil pH between 4.5–5.5; blueberries are very sensitive to high pH.
  - If you suspect pH is creeping up (yellowing new leaves, poor growth), test again and consider a small application of elemental sulfur or acidifying fertilizer.
- Mulch management:
  - Keep pine bark mulch 2–3 inches deep around the base but not touching trunks to prevent rot.
  - Replenish annually as it breaks down; this also keeps roots cool and moist.

2. Watering strategy (drip system)
- Frequency:
  - Blueberries need consistent moisture, especially during flowering and fruit set.
  - With drip irrigation in raised beds, aim for about 1–1.5 inches of water per week total (rain + irrigation). Adjust based on heat and soil type.
- How to check:
  - Insert a soil probe or screwdriver into the root zone; it should go in easily but not be soggy.
  - Use moisture sensors if available, or place small cups near plants to measure actual water delivery from emitters.
- Best practices:
  - Run drip lines early morning (reduces evaporation and disease risk).
  - Flush emitters weekly to prevent clogs; check for leaks or uneven flow.
  - Increase watering during hot spells, fruit development, and after pruning.

3. Fertilizer for next month (spring feeding)
- Type:
  - Use a fertilizer formulated specifically for blueberries or acid-loving plants. These are typically low in phosphorus and higher in nitrogen and sulfur.
  - Your plan to use ammonium sulfate is good; it supplies nitrogen and helps maintain acidity.
- Application timing & amounts (general guidelines):
  - Apply when new growth appears in spring, then again mid-season if needed.
  - Typical recommendation: about 1–2 pounds of ammonium sulfate per bush per year, split into 2–3 applications. Start with a smaller amount and adjust based on plant vigor and soil test results.
- What to avoid:
  - High-phosphorus fertilizers (can raise pH over time).
  - Fresh manure or unaged compost directly around roots; it can burn and alter pH unpredictably.

4. Pests to watch for
- Aphids:
  - Already seen you; neem oil is fine. Also try strong water sprays, beneficial insects (ladybugs), or insecticidal soap if infestations persist.
- Spider mites:
  - Look for stippled yellowing leaves and fine webbing in hot/dry periods. Use miticides or increase humidity/misting; remove heavily infested leaves.
- Slugs/snails:
  - Common on new growth and tender shoots, especially at night. Use bait stations, copper tape around plants, or hand-pick them.
- Japanese beetles:
  - Can skeletonize leaves and damage blossoms/fruit. Handpick in the morning when they’re sluggish; use traps cautiously (they can attract more).

5. Diseases to watch for
- Leaf spot / cane blight (fungal):
  - Look for dark spots on leaves, wilting shoots, or blackened canes. Improve airflow via pruning and spacing; remove infected material promptly. Fungicides are usually unnecessary if cultural practices are good.
- Root rot:
  - Signs include yellowing foliage, stunted growth, and mushy roots. Often caused by poor drainage or overwatering. Ensure your raised beds drain well and don’t let water pool around the base.

6. Quick seasonal checklist
- Now (spring):
  - Fertilize with ammonium sulfate; check drip system flow.
  - Prune dead/weak canes; apply fresh pine bark mulch if needed.
- Late spring:
  - Install bird netting when berries begin to swell.
  - Monitor for aphids, spider mites, and fungal spots.
- Summer:
  - Maintain consistent watering; flush drip lines weekly.
  - Hand-pick pests as needed; remove diseased leaves/canes.
- Fall/Winter:
  - Light pruning of dead wood; protect from severe cold if in a harsh zone (mulch base, avoid heavy snow load on branches).

Here are the top 10 tips to start a successful blueberry garden from scratch:

1. **Choose the right site**  
   - Full sun (6–8 hours/day) and good air circulation.  
   - Well-drained soil; avoid low spots where water pools. Raised beds or mounds work well in heavy clay.

2. **Match varieties to your climate & pollination needs**  
   - Select 3–5 different cultivars for staggered harvests and better fruit set.  
   - Many blueberries are self-fertile, but cross-pollinating varieties increase yield and quality.

3. **Test and adjust soil pH (critical)**  
   - Ideal pH: 4.5–5.5. Blueberries struggle outside this range.  
   - If your native soil is too alkaline, amend with peat moss, pine bark, or elemental sulfur before planting.

4. **Use the right soil mix**  
   - In raised beds, use a blend of:
     - 1/3 coarse pine bark or shredded hardwood mulch  
     - 1/3 peat moss or composted pine needles  
     - 1/3 perlite or coarse sand for drainage  
   - Avoid heavy clay; ensure the mix stays moist but never soggy.

5. **Plant at the right time**  
   - Bare-root: late winter to early spring while dormant.  
   - Container-grown: anytime, but avoid extreme heat/cold during transplanting.  
   - Dig a hole twice as wide as the root ball; keep soil level with or slightly above ground.

6. **Mulch heavily and correctly**  
   - Apply 3–4 inches of pine bark, pine straw, or shredded hardwood mulch around (not touching) each plant.  
   - Mulch keeps roots cool, retains moisture, suppresses weeds, and slowly acidifies soil as it breaks down.

7. **Water consistently—especially during key growth stages**  
   - Blueberries hate drought stress; they need steady moisture from planting through harvest.  
   - Drip irrigation or soaker hoses are ideal; aim for 1–2 inches of water per week total (rain + irrigation).

8. **Fertilize with acid-loving plant formulas**  
   - Use a low-phosphorus, high-nitrogen fertilizer designed for blueberries/acid-loving plants.  
   - Ammonium sulfate is excellent: apply in early spring and again mid-season if growth slows or leaves yellow.

9. **Prune annually to maintain shape and productivity**  
   - In late winter/early spring, remove dead/diseased canes and thin out weak ones.  
   - Keep an open center for airflow; aim for 10–15 strong canes per mature bush over time.

10. **Protect from birds and manage pests proactively**  
    - Install bird netting when berries begin to swell (secure edges so birds can’t slip under).  
    - Monitor regularly for aphids, spider mites, slugs/snails, and fungal issues; use neem oil, insecticidal soap, or hand-picking as needed.
