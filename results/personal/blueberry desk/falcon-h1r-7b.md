## 1. Drying – Prevent cracking before milling  

| Goal | Recommended method | Why it works for blueberry wood |
|------|-------------------|---------------------------------|
| **Even moisture loss, low risk of checking** | **Air‑dry in a climate‑controlled space** (45–60 % RH) for 2–3 weeks per inch of thickness, then finish with a kiln cycle if possible. | The wood is dense and fine‑grained; slow drying avoids rapid shrinkage that causes cracks. A dehumidifier or AC set to low humidity helps keep the rate steady. |
| **If you need faster results** | Small batch *kiln‑dry* (or use a portable dryer) for ≤ 2 inches thick pieces, then finish air‑drying. | Guarantees uniform moisture content and eliminates later warping/cracking risk. |
| **Check progress** | Use a moisture meter; aim for 10–12 % MC before milling. | Gives you confidence that the wood won’t split when you rip it. |

> **Tip:** Cut small, straight‑edged blanks first. They dry more uniformly and can be used as leg stock or top slabs while larger pieces finish later.

## 2. Straightening Small Irregular Pieces  

Your wood is up to 6 in thick but you only have 1–3 in sections that are bent/irregular. The goal is to flatten them into usable boards without excessive waste.

### A. Build a simple “log‑ripping jig” for the bandsaw  
1. **Frame:** Two parallel steel plates bolted together, distance set to your desired board thickness (e.g., ½ in).  
2. **Guide Fence:** Secure the band‑saw fence against one plate; use a sacrificial wood backer on the other side so the saw cuts cleanly across the entire width of the log.  
3. **Clamp the Log:** Place the bent piece flat on top of the lower plate, center it, and clamp lightly with C-clamps to hold it from moving while feeding.  

> This jig forces a straight cut through the middle of any curvature, turning a bowed section into a near‑flat board in one pass.

### B. Multi‑pass Ripping + Flattening Sequence  
| Pass | Tool | Cut thickness / orientation |
|------|------|-----------------------------|
| 1️⃣  | **Band Saw (ripping)** using the jig → cut a full‑length straight slab of your target thickness. | Remove as much material as you can; keep waste for other legs or trim pieces. |
| 2️⃣  | **Hand plane or jointer** (if available) to flatten each side and remove minor highs/lows left by the band saw. | Planes work well on dense wood if set sharp, but use a light pass to avoid gouging. |
| 3️⃣  | **Router with straight bit** for fine smoothing & edge cleanup. | Quick finish and adds consistency across glued‑up panels. |

### C. Alternative when the bandsaw can’t rip cleanly  
- **Crosscut first:** Saw short, straight sections (≈ 3–4 in) then glue them back together after flattening each piece separately. This reduces curvature in one board and allows you to use a table saw for ripping later if you acquire one.

## 3. Building the Minimalist Desk with Limited Wood  

### A. Leg Design  
- **Use straight‑grained blanks from your first rip passes.** Each leg can be ≈ 2 × 1½ in cross‑section, tapered slightly at the bottom for stability.
- **Joints:** Mortise‑and‑tenon on both ends of each leg and glue into a single rectangular frame. The tenon width should match the mortise depth (≈ ¾ in) to keep the legs upright without offset.

### B. Table Top Construction  
1. **Glue‑up slabs:** Arrange three or four pre‑flattened slabs side‑by‑side, leaving a small gap (½–⅜ in) between each for expansion; fill with shims later if needed.
2. **Edge finishing:** Router the outer edges of the glued block to a uniform thickness (e.g., 1¼ in) and then plane the faces again to remove any glue squeeze.
3. **Support frame:** The legs sit on top plates that are screwed or dowelled into the underside of the glued‑up slab, creating a solid support without visible offsets.

### C. Aprons & Stability  
- Keep aprons thin (¼–⅜ in) and glue them directly to the long sides of the table top after the surface is sanded smooth.
- Consider reinforcing the corners with pocket‑hole joinery or a few biscuits—these add strength without adding visible hardware.

### D. Waste Management  
- **Recycle scrap:** Cut off excess material from ripping into thin strips; glue them together for decorative side panels or small drawer fronts if you ever decide to expand later.
- **Don’t over‑cut:** Use the bandsaw jig to keep each cut as close to the target width as possible—extra waste can be turned into leg stock rather than discarded.

## 4. Grain Orientation Decision  

| Option | Appearance | Structural considerations | Wood usage efficiency |
|--------|------------|---------------------------|-----------------------|
| **Face‑grain** (long grain parallel to table top) | Shows the natural, elegant growth pattern across the surface. Good for a high‑end aesthetic on a small desk. | Strongest flexural strength when load runs perpendicular to grain; however, shear along grain is weaker—still fine for desk weight. | Uses whole boards as cut slabs → minimal waste. Best if you can keep each slab relatively large. |
| **Edge‑grain** (long grain vertical on the face) | Gives a sleek, modern look; the surface is smoother and less patterned. | Slightly higher resistance to splitting along the top because the load is across many growth rings. | Requires cutting boards into thinner slices (≈ ½–¾ in thick). Increases waste but can be compensated by using off‑cuts from leg stock as filler strips. |
| **End‑grain** (face made of small end‑grain squares) | Very striking; each square shows the full growth ring pattern—great for a statement piece. | End grain is naturally more resistant to splitting, but the surface can be harder to finish uniformly and may feel rougher unless hand‑sanded and sealed carefully. | Consumes the most material per finished area because every visible square must come from an end of a board; not ideal when wood is scarce. Consider using veneers or inlay only on key visual panels if you want that look without losing bulk.

### Recommendation for your situation  
- **Primary top:** Use face‑grain slabs (the largest pieces) to maximize usable surface area. The desk will be visually warm and highlight the wood’s fine grain.
- **Accent side or apron panels:** If you have excess narrow strips, apply edge‑grain orientation for a clean vertical line that complements the main top. This uses material that would otherwise be waste.

## 6. Safety & Quality Tips  

- **Blade speed:** Keep the bandsaw feed slow when cutting dense blueberry wood; a higher RPM can cause chatter and tear‑out.
- **Grain direction:** When ripping, let the saw cut *against* the grain for cleaner cuts on this hardwood. Use a back fence to keep the blade centered.
- **Clamp pressure:** For glued‑up panels, apply even clamping pressure—excess force can squeeze out moisture and cause delamination later.
- **Final check:** After sanding, test the desk by placing a few kilograms on it; if you notice any sagging at the leg mortise or apron seam, reinforce with an additional dowel or pocket‑hole.
