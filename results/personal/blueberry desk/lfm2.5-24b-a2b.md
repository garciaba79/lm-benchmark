### 1. Drying Blueberry Wood
Blueberry is a dense, slow‑grown hardwood that dries slowly and can crack if moisture leaves too quickly from its interior.

- **Stack with spacers:** Use ¼–½ in. stickers between layers to promote air circulation.
- **Control the drying rate:** Keep the stack out of direct sun but sheltered from rain. A slow, natural dry will minimize checks; a low‑temp kiln is ideal if you have access to one.
- **Target moisture content:** Aim for 8–10 % before milling. Test with a moisture meter when possible.
- **End sealing:** Apply wax, paint, or a commercial end‑grain sealant to all ends after cutting—this slows moisture exchange and reduces the risk of cracks during gluing.

### 2. Milling Irregular Small Pieces (1–3 in. thick)
Your custom rig will help rip round wood into straighter stock, but the irregular shapes need careful handling:

- **Rip to straight strips first:** Keep the natural bend or curve intact until you decide whether it can be straightened; then trim curves with a hand jig or a small bandsaw.
- **Blade choice:** Use a sharp carbide‑tipped blade (e.g., 21 TPI) for clean cuts on the fine grain of blueberry, which is prone to tear‑out.
- **Crosscut sleds/push sticks:** Set the blade just above the wood surface for minimal tear‑out when cross‑cutting at the rig’s base.
- **Edge preparation:** Joint or plane all ripped strips flat and square; this is essential before gluing up multiple pieces together.

You want a clean design without drawers, so plan for simplicity and material efficiency:

- **Top:** If you can obtain one or two wide boards (or make your own from glued‑up strips), face‑grain will give the best visual effect. The fine grain of blueberry shows beautifully when cut parallel to the growth rings.
- **Apron & legs:** Keep these components thin—2–3 in. apron, simple square or tapered legs from solid sections. Use pocket‑hole joinery (e.g., Kreg) plus glue for strong, hidden connections that don’t clutter the minimalist aesthetic.

### 4. Grain Orientation: Face‑, Edge-, or End‑Grain
| Orientation | Pros | Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| **Face‑grain** | Most beautiful grain visibility; solid slab look on the tabletop. | Requires a wide enough piece to avoid too many seams; internal weakness across narrow widths if cut from thin slats. |
| **Edge‑grain** | Continuous grain lines when assembled edge‑to‑edge; more material‑efficient for limited stock. | Visible glue line between strips, but clean and modern. Best for a wider tabletop made from multiple narrow boards. |
| **End‑grain** | Strongest surface joint (e.g., where legs attach); resists wear at contact points. | Uses the most wood per joint; harder to work with, and not ideal for visible surfaces on the top. |

**Recommendation:**  
- If you can mill a wide slab from your best‑quality blueberry pieces, use **face‑grain** on the tabletop for its aesthetic appeal.  
- Otherwise, construct the tabletop from narrower strips glued edge‑to‑edge using **edge‑grain**—this yields a continuous pattern while minimizing waste and still looks minimalist.
