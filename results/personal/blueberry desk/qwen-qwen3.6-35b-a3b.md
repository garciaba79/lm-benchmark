### 1. Working with Blueberry Wood
*   **Density & Hardness:** Blueberry wood is exceptionally hard and dense (often comparable to hickory or maple, sometimes harder). This means:
    *   **Tool Wear:** Use sharp, high-quality carbide-tipped blades and bits. Dull tools will burn the wood or require excessive force.
    *   **Pre-Drilling:** Always pre-drill for screws or nails to prevent splitting.
    *   **Finishing:** The fine grain will take polish beautifully, but ensure surfaces are perfectly smooth before applying finish, as imperfections will be highly visible.
*   **Drying & Cracking:**
    *   **Slow Drying is Critical:** Due to its density, moisture moves slowly through the wood. Kiln drying is ideal, but if air drying, use stickers generously and ensure excellent airflow. Expect a much longer drying time than typical hardwoods.
    *   **End Grain Sealing:** Seal the end grains of all logs/pieces with wax or commercial end-grain sealer immediately after cutting to slow moisture loss from the ends and reduce checking/cracking.
    *   **Monitoring:** Monitor for cracks during drying. If cracks appear, they may need to be filled later or accepted as part of the wood’s character.

### 2. Milling Challenges & Solutions
*   **Small, Irregular Pieces:**
    *   **Bandsaw Rig:** Your custom rig for ripping round wood is essential. Ensure it has a fence that can be adjusted precisely and a mechanism to hold the log firmly without crushing it.
    *   **Live Center:** If your bandsaw table allows, consider using a live center or a steady rest for longer pieces to prevent vibration and improve cut accuracy.
    *   **Jigs:** Build simple jigs for resawing or cutting to consistent widths if your bandsaw’s fence isn’t precise enough for your needs.
*   **Time-Consuming Milling:**
    *   **Batch Processing:** Group similar pieces together (e.g., all pieces of similar thickness) to minimize jig adjustments.
    *   **Accept Imperfections:** Since you’re making a minimalist desk, some natural edges or slight irregularities might be acceptable or even desirable if planned for. However, for structural parts (legs, aprons, top surface), strive for straightness.
    *   **Waste Management:** Collect and store sawdust and small offcuts. They can be used for:
        *   Burning (if dry enough).
        *   Composting (small amounts).
        *   Small craft projects (if you have other interests).
        *   Donating to local farmers or gardeners (as mulch or biomass).

### 3. Desk Design & Wood Supply

*   **Glue-Ups & Waste:**

*   **No Drawers:** This simplifies construction and saves wood/materials. Consider alternative storage solutions (a separate cabinet, shelf, or basket) if needed.

### 4. Grain Orientation for the Desk Top
This is a crucial aesthetic and structural decision:

*   **Face-Grain (Flat-Sawn):**
    *   **Appearance:** Shows the broadest grain pattern, often with cathedral arches. Most common and generally most economical use of wood (less waste during milling).
    *   **Stability:** Prone to cupping if not properly dried and glued. However, with proper glue-up and adequate thickness, it can be stable.
    *   **Recommendation:** Often the best choice for maximizing wood yield and achieving a classic look. Ensure boards are well-seasoned and glued with care to minimize cupping.

*   **Edge-Grain (Quarter-Sawn or Rift-Sawn):**
    *   **Appearance:** Shows straight, vertical grain lines. Highly stable and resistant to cupping. Can be more labor-intensive to mill from round logs (requires quarter-sawing or rift-sawing, which yields less board footage).
    *   **Stability:** Excellent dimensional stability.
    *   **Recommendation:** Ideal if stability is your top priority and you can afford the waste. The straight grain can look very clean and minimalist.

*   **End-Grain:**
    *   **Appearance:** Shows the concentric rings of the tree. Very distinctive, durable, and self-healing surface (great for cutting boards). Can be visually striking but also busy.
    *   **Stability:** Highly stable if properly constructed.
    *   **Recommendation:** **Generally not recommended for a desk top** unless you have a specific artistic vision. It’s extremely wasteful (requires cutting across the log), difficult to mill flat, and can feel less "substantial" or more like a butcher block under the hands. It also requires precise joinery to prevent gaps.

**Recommendation:** For a minimalist desk using limited wild-harvested wood, **Face-Grain** is likely the most practical choice, offering a good balance of aesthetics, wood yield, and manageable stability (with proper drying/glue-up). **Edge-Grain** is a strong second choice if you prioritize stability and can accept the higher waste. Avoid End-Grain unless you have abundant wood and a specific design goal.

### Additional Considerations
*   **Testing:** If possible, test your drying and milling process on a small, less critical piece first.
*   **Joinery:** Since you’re using hard wood, consider strong joinery like mortise-and-tenon or dowels for the legs and aprons. Avoid relying solely on screws in end grain.
*   **Finish:** Choose a finish that enhances the wood’s natural beauty without obscuring the grain. Oil (e.g., tung oil, linseed oil) or a thin, durable varnish/polyurethane are good options. Test on scrap wood first.
*   **Documentation:** Keep notes on the drying time, milling process, and any issues encountered. This will be invaluable if you work with Blueberry wood again.

This project is a testament to resourcefulness and craftsmanship. Embrace the challenges as part of the process, and focus on creating a functional, beautiful piece that honors the unique character of your wild-harvested wood. Good luck!
