### 1. Grain Orientation Analysis for Blueberry Wood

#### **Option A: Face-Grain (Traditional)**
*   **Aesthetic:** Shows the broadest surface of the rings. If your blueberry wood has distinct growth rings or color variation (which it often does, with a pale sapwood and darker heartwood), this will highlight those patterns.
*   **Material Efficiency:** **Low.** This is the most wasteful option for short, thick rounds. To get a flat, wide slab, you must mill away a significant amount of material from the curved sides of the 1–3" thick pieces. Given your limited supply, this might be too costly.
*   **Stability:** Face-grain surfaces are more susceptible to cupping and warping over time, though blueberry is less prone to warping than many species, it is still prone to *checking* (cracking) if dried improperly.
*   **Verdict:** Avoid if material is extremely scarce. Only choose this if you have enough waste material to spare and want to showcase ring patterns.

#### **Option B: Edge-Grain (Bookmatched or Straight)**
*   **Aesthetic:** The grain runs perpendicular to the face of the desk. This creates a more uniform, linear appearance. It highlights the density and fine texture of the wood rather than ring patterns.
*   **Material Efficiency:** **Medium.** Still requires milling the sides flat, but you can orient the pieces so the "face" of the board is the narrow side of the round. This can save material compared to trying to get wide slabs.
*   **Stability:** Edge-grain is more dimensionally stable than face-grain. It cups less.
*   **Verdict:** A solid, safe choice. It looks clean and modern, fitting your minimalist design.

#### **Option C: End-Grain (Butcher Block Style)**
*   **Aesthetic:** The grain runs perpendicular to the top surface. You see the cross-section of the tree rings. For blueberry, this will show the annual growth rings in a circular or radial pattern. It is highly distinctive and looks like a "target" or "tree slice" pattern when assembled.
*   **Material Efficiency:** **High (in terms of usable area per piece).** You can mill the short, thick pieces into cubes or small squares. Even small, irregular pieces (1–3" thick) can be milled into blocks that fit together. **This is the best way to use irregular, bent, or short pieces.**
*   **Stability:** End-grain is the *least* stable in terms of resisting swelling/shrinking with humidity. It can cup significantly if not properly supported and finished. However, for a desk, this is manageable with proper finishing and construction.
*   **Durability:** End-grain is excellent for holding surfaces (like a cutting board) but can feel "spongy" or porous compared to face-grain. It requires more finishing to seal.
*   **Verdict:** **Recommended for your situation.** It allows you to use the most difficult, short, irregular pieces. It creates a unique, high-contrast visual that highlights the "wild" nature of the blueberry wood. It turns a weakness (short, irregular pieces) into a strength (unique mosaic pattern).

### 2. Recommendation: **End-Grain Construction**

Given your constraints:
1.  **Limited Quantity:** End-grain lets you use the smallest, most irregular off-cuts.
2.  **Irregular Shapes:** Short, bent pieces are easy to mill into small, straight blocks for end-grain.
3.  **Aesthetic:** It’s minimalist yet highly detailed. The fine grain and hard density of blueberry will create a very durable, distinctive surface.
4.  **Waste Reduction:** While glue-ups produce waste, end-grain blocks require less precision milling than long, flat slabs.

### 3. Critical Process Steps for Blueberry Wood

#### **Drying (Crucial to Prevent Cracking)**
*   **Problem:** You noted it cracks if not properly dried.
*   **Solution:**
    *   **Air Dry Slowly:** Stack the milled pieces with stickers (spacers) in a shaded, dry area. **Do not** leave them in the sun or near heat.
    *   **Wrap in Plastic:** For small pieces (1–3"), wrap them individually in plastic wrap (like Saran) with a small amount of moisture (damp paper towel) inside to slow down the drying rate. This prevents surface checking.
    *   **Seal the Ends:** Apply end-sealer or wax to the cut ends immediately after milling to prevent rapid moisture loss from the end grain.
    *   **Patience:** Blueberry is dense. It dries slowly. Rushing it will cause cracks. Wait until the moisture content is below 8–10% before gluing.

#### **Milling the Small, Irregular Pieces**
*   **Bandsaw Rig:** Your custom rig is a great idea. For small, bent pieces:
    *   Use a **thin kerf blade** to reduce heat buildup.
    *   Use **fingers** on the infeed side to keep the piece straight.
    *   Mill **slowly**. Blueberry is hard and can kick back if the blade binds.
    *   **Resawing:** If you have 6" thick trunks, resaw them into 1–2" thick slabs first. Then, if choosing end-grain, cross-cut these slabs into blocks.

#### **Gluing Up**
*   **Glue Choice:** Use a **PVA glue** (Titebond III or similar) for the blocks. Epoxy is overkill and harder to clean. CA (cyanoacrylate) can be used for quick clamping or small repairs.
*   **Clamping:** Use many clamps. End-grain glue-ups require heavy, even pressure. Consider using **caul pieces** (scrap wood) between the blocks and clamps to distribute pressure evenly and prevent crushing.
*   **Alignment:** This is the hardest part. Use **dowels** or **pins** to keep the blocks aligned during clamping. You can also use **wedges** (small slivers of wood) to pull the blocks tight and keep them aligned.

#### **Finishing**
*   **Sanding:** Start with 80-grit, work up to 220-grit. End-grain is porous, so sanding will reveal more grain.
*   **Finish:** Use a **oil-based finish** (Danish oil, tung oil, or linseed oil). These penetrate the end-grain and enhance the density. Polyurethane can look plasticky on end-grain.
*   **Sealing:** Apply multiple coats. End-grain absorbs finish quickly.
