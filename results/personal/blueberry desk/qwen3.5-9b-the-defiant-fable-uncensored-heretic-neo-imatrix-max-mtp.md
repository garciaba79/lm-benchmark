Great setup—blueberry wood is a fantastic choice for a minimalist desk, especially with that fine grain and stability. Here’s a concise plan that addresses milling, joinery, glue-up waste, and grain orientation given your constraints.

1) Milling strategy (round to straight, minimize waste)
- Use the bandsaw rig:
  - Rip rounds into quarters or halves first; this reduces bending risk compared to trying to rip full rounds in one pass.
  - Keep cuts shallow and steady; let the wood support itself as much as possible.
- Flatten faces early:
  - After ripping, joint/flatten at least two opposite faces on each piece (planer or belt sander). This gives you stable reference surfaces for layout and gluing.
- Sort by shape/defect:
  - Group pieces with similar thickness and curvature so they can be paired in glue-ups; this reduces the number of different laminations needed.

2) Glue-up approach (maximize yield, minimize waste)
- Build dimensional lumber via edge-gluing:
  - For a desk top that’s, say, 1–1.5" thick, stack multiple 3/4" or 1" slabs and glue them edge-to-edge. This uses more of each irregular slab than trying to cut full thickness from one piece.
- Use biscuits/dowels sparingly:
  - For a minimalist look, rely on tight fit + clamping; add biscuits only where alignment is critical (e.g., apron rails).
- Plan glue-ups around your pieces:
  - Map out which slabs go into the top vs legs/apron. Put the most visually interesting faces in the top; use less attractive sides for structural parts or hidden areas.

3) Desk structure suggestions (simple, stable, no drawers)
- Top:
  - Glued-up slab(s), edge-glued to reach desired width/length. Keep thickness modest (1–1.5") so it’s not overkill for blueberry wood.
- Apron:
  - Simple rectangular frame around the top edges; attach with mortise-and-tenon or strong dowels + glue. Avoid offsets if you’re short on material.
- Legs:
  - Straight square or slightly tapered legs, joined to apron with mortise-and-tenon or box joints. Keep them simple and symmetrical for a clean look.

4) Grain orientation options (aesthetic vs practical)
Given limited wood, here are the three main choices:

- Face-grain top (most common):
  - Pros: Shows full grain pattern; classic desk look; stable if properly dried/glued.
  - Cons: Uses your best faces on the top only; requires careful matching of patterns across seams.
  - Best for: A showpiece surface that highlights blueberry’s fine grain.

- Edge-grain top (slabs glued face-down):
  - Pros: More stable against cupping; interesting linear pattern from growth rings; uses slab faces as “hidden” sides.
  - Cons: Less dramatic than face-grain; seams can be more visible if not matched well.
  - Best for: A subtle, modern look with strong stability.

- End-grain top (rare for a desk):
  - Pros: Unique texture and pattern; very distinctive.
  - Cons: More prone to checking/cracking if not perfectly dried/glued; visually busy; uses up the best faces on structural parts or hidden areas.
  - Best for: A bold statement piece, but only if you’re confident in drying and gluing end-grain laminates.

Recommendation:
- Go with a face-grain top made from edge-glued slabs. It gives the most visual impact while keeping stability high and using your best faces where they matter most. Match grain patterns across seams for continuity; keep seam lines subtle or align them to run parallel to the desk’s length.

5) Drying and finishing (critical for blueberry wood)
- Dry slowly:
  - Blueberry can crack if dried too fast. Aim for a gradual moisture content drop, ideally in a controlled environment.
- Condition before milling:
  - If possible, let it acclimate to your shop humidity for several weeks before heavy milling and gluing.
- Finish:
  - A penetrating oil or thin film finish (e.g., Danish oil, tung oil, or a light poly) will accentuate the fine grain without adding too much thickness.
