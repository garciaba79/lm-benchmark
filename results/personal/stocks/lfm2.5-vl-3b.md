**Options trading explained for someone who already knows how to buy and sell stocks**

---

### 1. What is an option?

An **option** is a contract that gives you the *right* (but not the obligation) to buy or sell a specific stock at a price set in the future. Think of it as a “future‑date” purchase agreement for the stock.

- **Call option:** You have the right to **buy** the stock at a fixed price (the strike price).  
- **Put option:** You have the right to **sell** the stock at that same strike price.

---

### 2. How does it differ from buying or selling a stock?

| Aspect | Buying / Selling Stock | Option |
|--------|-----------------------|--------|
| **Ownership** | You own shares of the company. | You don’t own anything; you hold a contract. |
| **Risk** | Limited to the price movement of the stock (up or down). | Unlimited if the underlying moves far from the strike price. |
| **Leverage** | Low – you invest only the purchase price. | High – you can control many shares with a small premium. |
| **Time frame** | Immediate (once you buy/sell). | Can be expire in minutes, days, weeks, or months. |

---

### 3. Key terms you’ll hear

- **Strike price:** The price at which the option can be exercised.  
- **Premium:** The fee paid for the right to trade; it’s usually a percentage of the underlying stock’s price.  
- **Expiration date:** When the contract must be exercised or becomes worthless.  
- **In‑the‑money (ITM):** The option is profitable if the market moves in your direction.  
- **Out‑of‑the‑money (OTM):** You’ll lose the premium, but you can still profit later if the price changes dramatically.

---

### 4. How to trade an option

1. **Choose a stock** and decide whether you want a call or put.  
2. **Pick a strike price** that matches your expectation of future price movement (e.g., if you think the stock will rise, you might buy a call at a lower strike).  
3. **Pay the premium** – this is your upfront cost.  
4. **Monitor the market.** If the underlying moves toward or away from the strike, you can decide to:  
   - **Exercise** (buy/sell) if it’s profitable, or  
   - **Sell the option** back to a broker for a profit equal to the premium minus any loss on the underlying.

---

### 5. Simple example

- You own 100 shares of **Apple** and think they’ll go up $2 next month.  
- You buy a **call option** with a strike price of **$150** for **$10 per share**.  
- The premium is $1,000 total.  

If Apple closes at **$160**, you can exercise the call and sell your shares at $160, netting a profit of **$60** (the $10 premium minus the $100 cost to buy the option). If it closes at **$140**, the option expires worthless and you lose only the $1,000 premium.

---

### 6. Why people use options

- **Hedging:** Protect yourself against a bad move in your stock position.  
- **Speculating:** Bet on short‑term price swings without owning shares.  
- **Leverage:** Amplify returns (and risks) with a small premium.

---

### 7. Risks to keep in mind

- **Unlimited loss** if the underlying moves far from your strike price.  
- **Premium cost** – you pay for the right, not ownership.  
- **Complexity** – requires understanding of time decay (theta), volatility, and market direction.

---

### 8. Bottom line

Options are a way to *trade* on a stock’s future price without actually buying or selling it today. They let you profit from both upward and downward moves, but they also expose you to greater risk because the potential loss is theoretically unlimited. If you’re comfortable with that, options can be a powerful tool in your investment toolkit—especially when combined with other strategies like long‑term holding or shorting stocks.
