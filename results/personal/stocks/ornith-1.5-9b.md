# Options Trading Explained

Since you already know how stocks work (you own actual shares), let's build on that. The key mental shift is this: **an option isn't a share of stock—it's a contract about a share.** You're buying the *right* to do something with a stock, not owning it directly.

## What an Option Actually Is

An option gives you the right (but never the obligation) to buy or sell a specific stock at a set price, before a certain date. Think of it like a lease on a car—you pay upfront for the *option* to buy it later at today's price, and if your plans change, you just walk away instead of being forced through with the deal.

## The Two Basic Types

- **Call option** = the right to **buy** the stock (you're betting the price goes *up*)
- **Put option** = the right to **sell** the stock (you're betting the price goes *down*)

## Key Terms You'll See

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Strike price** | The agreed-upon price you can buy/sell at |
| **Premium** | What you pay for the option contract |
| **Expiration date** | When the option must be used or becomes worthless |
| **Underlying asset** | The stock the option is based on |
| **Contract size** | Usually 100 shares per contract |

## A Concrete Example (Buying a Call)

Say Stock XYZ trades at **$100**. You think it'll rise over the next few months. Instead of buying 100 shares for $10,000, you buy one call option with a strike price of $100 and pay a premium of **$5/share = $500 total** (one contract).

- **If XYZ rises to $150:** You can exercise your right to buy at $100 and sell at $150. That's $50 × 100 shares = $5,000 gain, minus your $500 premium = **$4,500 profit on a $500 investment** (9x return).
- **If XYZ stays flat or falls:** The option expires worthless and you lose only the **$500 premium**—your maximum loss.

This is where options shine: they give you **leverage** (control 100 shares with less money) while capping your downside at what you paid.

## Why People Trade Options

- **Leverage:** Big price moves on a smaller amount of capital
- **Hedging:** Protecting stock holdings you already own (e.g., buying puts as insurance against a drop)
- **Income:** Selling options to collect premiums
- **Speculation:** Betting on direction without committing full share prices

## The Main Differences From Stocks

1. **They expire.** Options have an end date; stocks don't. After expiration, they're either exercised or worthless.
2. **Time decay works against you.** An option loses value as it approaches expiration (called "theta"), even if the stock doesn't move much. This is why buying options can be risky—you need the stock to move *enough* before expiry.
3. **You can buy OR sell them.** Selling ("writing") an option means you collect the premium upfront but take on potential obligation, which carries larger risk than simply buying.

## One More Concept: Moneyness

- **In-the-money:** The option is already worth something based on current price (e.g., a call when the stock is above your strike)
- **At-the-money:** Strike price roughly equals current price
- **Out-of-money:** Exercising would lose money right now, so it's only valuable for its *time* premium

---

**Bottom line:** Options are contracts that let you express views on a stock's future movement with leverage and defined risk (when buying). They're powerful but more complex than stocks because of expiration dates, time decay, and the fact that you can be assigned an obligation if you sell them.
