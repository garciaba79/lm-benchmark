Great—since you know stocks, here’s a concise map of options:

- What an option is:
  - A contract giving you the right (but not obligation) to buy or sell a stock at a set price by a certain date.
  - You pay a premium upfront for that right.

- The two basic types:
  - Call option: Right to BUY the stock at a strike price before expiration.
    - Profitable when the market price > strike + premium you paid.
  - Put option: Right to SELL the stock at a strike price before expiration.
    - Profitable when the market price < strike − premium you received (if sold) or < strike − premium you paid (if bought).

- Key terms:
  - Underlying: The stock (or ETF, index, etc.) the option is based on.
  - Strike price: The fixed price at which you can exercise your right.
  - Expiration date: When the contract ends; after that it’s worthless if not exercised.
  - Premium: The price of the option itself.
    - Call premium rises when stock goes up, volatility rises, or time to expiration increases (all else equal).
    - Put premium behaves oppositely relative to stock price.

- How you can trade options (simple positions):
  - Buy a call: Bullish bet. You profit if the stock rises above your strike + premium by expiration. Max loss = premium paid.
  - Sell (write) a call: Bearish/neutral income play. You collect premium but must deliver shares at strike if assigned. Risk is large/unlimited if you don’t own the shares and price surges.
  - Buy a put: Bearish bet. Profit if stock falls below your strike − premium by expiration. Max loss = premium paid.
  - Sell (write) a put: Bullish/neutral income play. You collect premium but must buy shares at strike if assigned. Risk is large if price crashes.

- Why use options instead of just stocks?
  - Leverage: Small premium can control many shares; big moves in the stock create outsized P&L on the option.
  - Defined risk (when buying): Max loss = premium paid.
  - Income/strategy building: Selling options, spreads, etc., to generate income or hedge positions.

- Two important concepts:
  - Time decay (theta): As expiration approaches, all else equal, option value erodes—hurts buyers, helps sellers.
  - Implied volatility (IV): Market’s expectation of future movement; higher IV → more expensive options.
